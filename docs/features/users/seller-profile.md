The page a seller has of their own, read by a buyer partway through deciding on one of their listings. It answers one question — who is on the other side of this, and what else do they have — so it is a trust surface and a second storefront rather than an account view.

It is reached by the seller's name on a listing's seller card, and nowhere else: there is no seller directory and no sidebar entry, so the page is only ever entered sideways. An account with no handle has no address and its name stays plain text.

Its address is the seller's handle — `/seller/martar`. A handle is the only public name an account carries. A handle its owner has since changed stops resolving, so a link shared under an old one no longer opens the page.

It is public: a visitor with no account reaches it, and no sign-in stands between arriving and opening a listing.

A handle nobody holds, a suspended account, and a closed one all read as the same statement: no seller here, the address may be wrong, and nothing further is said about it. A suspended seller is off the marketplace, so their public page is off it too. A restricted account keeps its page — a restriction limits what someone may do, it does not take away who they are.

## What the page holds

An identity band across the top, then the listings below it. The band frames the page and pays for itself once; the listings are what a buyer came for and get the width.

The band holds the seller's avatar, their name, their handle, and how long they have been on Mercato. An account opened within the last thirty days is marked as new, so a buyer finding no sales behind a seller is given the reason instead of guessing at one. A name with nothing to write falls back to the handle — never to the email address, which belongs to its owner alone.

At the far end of the band sit two counts: what is on offer, and what has sold. They are the two numbers that place a seller, so they summarise the band rather than sitting beside the name as a claim. A count of none is shown as such, since hiding it would read as concealment.

A rating, a review count, a response time, and a dispatch time are facts about the same subject and join that line as the features producing them arrive.

Nothing on the page is a control over the seller. There is no follow, no message, no share, and no report — the only thing a visitor can press is a listing.

The trail above the page is one level deep, because a seller sits under nothing.

## Which listings appear

What is on offer leads the page, and what has sold sits below it, separated by a rule and a quieter heading. A listing on offer is the only kind a buyer can act on, so it carries the visual weight; a sold listing is evidence that the seller delivers, which must stay findable without competing with what is for sale.

A card for something on offer opens that listing. A sold card opens nothing — it is a record, and its own page would refuse a visitor anyway — and it is dimmed and badged as sold, so nothing about it reads as purchasable. Its price stays visible, because what a seller's items went for is part of the same evidence.

Draft and paused listings appear nowhere: not in either group, and not in the counts. A draft was never public, and a paused listing has been deliberately taken out of public view. The count of what is on offer can therefore differ from what the seller sees in their own view of everything they have listed.

The sold group shows four at most, with a control to open the rest in place, so a long history cannot push what is for sale off the screen.

## What each state shows

| The seller has | The page shows |
|---|---|
| Listings on offer and sales behind them | Both groups, sold below on offer |
| Listings on offer, nothing sold yet | The on-offer group alone; the sold section is absent rather than empty |
| Nothing on offer, everything sold | The on-offer heading kept, with a stated reason under it, and the full sold group below |
| Nothing either way | One calm empty state, and no action — there is nothing a visitor could usefully do about it |

Everything having sold is a good outcome and is stated as one. Nothing about that state reads as an error.

## The seller's own view

A seller opening their own profile gets exactly what a visitor gets: the same listings, the same counts, and no editing affordance. A profile that differed for its owner could not be used to check what buyers see, and account settings are a page of their own.
